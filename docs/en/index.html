Knights of Columbus — Chronology

Chronology of the Knights of Columbus — the founding, the growth of the Order, its dealings with the Holy See, and the anti-Catholic campaigns against it in the United States, including the fabricated "bogus oath" and the Ku Klux Klan

An open, source-referenced chronology of the Knights of Columbus, the Catholic fraternal benefit society founded in New Haven in 1882 — its institutional history, its relations with the popes, its public campaigns, and the documented conflict between the Order and organized anti-Catholicism in the United States, from the fabricated Fourth Degree oath of 1912 to the second Ku Klux Klan and its echoes.

About

Work-in-progress dataset compiled from public sources (see references). Institutional events lean on the Order's own centennial timeline and are attributed as the Order's account where the Order is crediting itself; figures for membership, insurance and charitable giving are time-stamped, because they change every year. The anti-Catholic-conflict thread rests on period primary documents where they were reached — the Congressional Record, contemporary newspapers, the Supreme Court's opinion, archival scans of the books themselves — and on named scholarship elsewhere. The fabricated "bogus oath" is documented here as a fabrication: that finding belongs to the House of Representatives (1913) and the courts, and the dataset cites the acts, not the slur. Day-level dates that rest on a single tertiary source are flagged with a ? mark. Contested characterizations are attributed to their authors; where sources disagree — the February 1882 founding day, the founding rationale of the Knights of Peter Claver — the dataset records the disagreement instead of resolving it. Corrections against primary sources are welcome.

What it is
A Catholic fraternal benefit society of lay men, founded in New Haven, Connecticut, in 1881–82 — a mutual-aid and insurance organization built around the principles of charity, unity and fraternity, with patriotism added with the Fourth Degree in 1900.[1] [8] [5]
Founder
Blessed Michael J. McGivney (1852–1890), a 29-year-old curate of St. Mary's Church, New Haven, son of Irish immigrants; he died at 38 and was beatified on 31 October 2020.[12] [13]
First meeting
2 October 1881, in the basement of St. Mary's Church, New Haven — a group of laymen convened by Fr. McGivney to plan a Catholic mutual-benefit society.[1] [9]
Charter
29 March 1882, by act of the Connecticut General Assembly — the date the Order marks as Founder's Day.[9] [1]
Original purpose
The 1913 Catholic Encyclopedia states it: to develop a practical Catholicity among members, to promote Catholic education and charity, and through its insurance department to furnish at least temporary financial aid to the families of deceased members.[8]
Governance
A Supreme Knight and Supreme Council/Board of Directors, headquartered in New Haven, Connecticut; local units are councils. Fourteen men have held the office of Supreme Knight since 1882.[5] [2]
Scale (2025, per the Order)
About 2.2 million members in some 17,000 councils; $124 billion of life insurance in force; the Order reports over $200 million donated and 48 million volunteer hours in 2025. Figures are the Order's own and change yearly.[2]
Scale (2024, reported)
At the 142nd Supreme Convention (August 2024) the Order reported 2.1 million members, 92,000 of them new that year, a record $190 million in charitable giving and 47 million volunteer hours.[15]
Expansion
Canada from 1897 (Montreal), Mexico and the Philippines from 1905, Panama and Cuba from 1909, Poland from 2006, South Korea from 2015 — the Order's own council-charter record.[1]
The "bogus oath"
A fabricated Fourth Degree oath circulated from 1912 to defame the Order as a murderous conspiracy. A committee of the U.S. House of Representatives found it spurious in 1913, and publishers who circulated it were convicted of criminal libel; the Klan of the 1920s circulated it anyway, and it resurfaced in the presidential campaigns of 1928 and 1960.[1] [25]

McGivney, the 29-year-old curate of St. Mary's Church, gathers a group of laymen in the parish basement to plan a Catholic mutual-benefit society — a lay-led organization to unite Catholic men and provide for the families of deceased members, at a time when, as the Order's own history and independent accounts both note, Catholic men were joining secret fraternal societies the Church disapproved of, and industrial work left widows and orphans without support.[1] [9] [5]

1900 1900-02-22 New York City, USA The Fourth Degree is exemplified for the first time — Eleven hundred Knights receive the new Fourth Degree, whose principle is patriotism, in New York City; 750 more follow in Boston that May. It is the Fourth Degree's ceremonial oath that the fabricators of the "bogus oath" would later claim to expose.[1]

Manila, Philippines / Mexico City, Mexico First councils in the Philippines and Mexico — Manila Council 1000 and Mexico City's Guadalupe Council 1050 are chartered, opening the Order's presence in Asia and Latin America, per the Order's timeline.[1]1905; the exact charter days of Manila Council 1000 and Guadalupe Council 1050 were not established in the sources reached.
1909 1909-11-07 Mobile, Alabama, USA The Knights of Peter Claver are founded — Four Josephite priests and three Black Catholic laymen found the Knights of Peter Claver, a separate Black Catholic fraternal order. Why a separate order was needed is told differently by different sources: the National Catholic Register writes that it spread where Knights of Columbus councils had excluded Black men through blackball admission votes, while BlackPast.org attributes the founding to the Josephites' concern about losing Black Catholics to the Elks and Masons, without mentioning the Knights of Columbus; the Claver order's own history page states no rationale. The dataset records the framings without resolving them.[16] [17] [18]
1910s
1912 ? Pennsylvania, USA A fabricated "Fourth Degree oath" enters a congressional election — During the contest for Pennsylvania's 7th district, a fabricated "oath" attributed to Fourth Degree Knights — pledging to hang, burn and strangle heretics — is circulated against the Democratic candidate Eugene C. Bonniwell, himself a Fourth Degree Knight. The winning Republican, Thomas S. Butler, stated for the record that he "did not believe in its truthfulness" and called the document spurious. Surviving broadsides of the fabrication are headed "Taken from the Congressional Record of February 15" — borrowing the authority of the very record that condemned it.[25] [52] [32]October–November 1912, during the congressional campaign; sources disagree on where the text first appeared — Time in 1928 wrote that it 'was circulated first in Chester County, Pa., about 1912', while Time in 1960 traced it to a tract mailed from Aurora, Missouri. Authorship is unknown per all sources reached.
1913 1913-02-15 Washington, D.C., USA A House committee brands the oath "spurious" — In the contested-election case of Bonniwell v. Butler, the House Committee on Elections No. 1 reports to the full House; the report, printed in the Congressional Record of 15 February 1913, reproduces the fabricated oath as an exhibit and Butler's repudiation of it. As quoted with page citations in later reprints, the committee declared it "cannot condemn too strongly the publication of the false and libelous article... which was the spurious Knights of Columbus Oath". This is the primary public debunking act, and the document later distributors would misrepresent as congressional endorsement.[25] [26] [34]
1913 1913-02-18 ? St. John's, Newfoundland First criminal-libel trial over the oath: Charles A. Swift — Charles A. Swift is tried for criminal libel in the Central District Court of St. John's for publishing and circulating the oath. Per the trial record as reprinted by the Order, Swift admitted the charge and expressed "very deep regret... that I should have been led to believe through false representations that this was the oath of the Knights of Columbus".[26] [27]18 February 1913 per the Order's 1914 pamphlet, which reprints the trial record; the Catholic Bulletin of St. Paul reported the arrest on 15 February. The pamphlet is the Order's own publication, and the account is attributed to it.
1913 1913-02-19 ? Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA Philadelphia arrests: the printer and the distributor — Printer Charles Megonegal and barber Clarence H. Stage are arrested for criminal libel and conspiracy over the bogus oath, on the affidavit of a Knight; a magistrate holds Megonegal in $2,000 bail and Stage in $1,000. At the hearing, per the period Catholic press and the Order's pamphlet, Megonegal said he had bought copies of the oath in bulk from The Menace, the anti-Catholic weekly of Aurora, Missouri, before printing his own.[27] [26]The Catholic Bulletin reports the arrests on 19 February 1913; the Order's pamphlet dates the holding-over to 20 February — likely arrest versus arraignment. Both dates recorded.

Waterville, Minnesota, USA The Morrisons convicted; the real Fourth Degree obligation enters a court record — A jury — with the town's Methodist minister as foreman, as the secular local press noted — unanimously convicts A. M. and G. E. Morrison, father and son, of criminal libel for charging that a local editor had subscribed to the alleged oath. Supreme Secretary William J. McGinley placed the real Fourth Degree obligation in evidence, in what the press of the day called the first opportunity the Order had been given to put the real obligation on a court record. Sentence: thirty days each.[28] [26]Mid-August 1914 — the Manchester Democrat of 19 August reprints the Telegraph-Herald item as 'last week'; the exact trial day was not pinned. The Order's pamphlet records the sentence and the September appeal.
1914 ? United States The Commission on Religious Prejudices is created — The Supreme Council institutes a Commission on Religious Prejudices — mandated, per the New Catholic Encyclopedia's account, "to study the causes, investigate conditions and suggest remedies for the religious prejudice that has been manifest through the press and rostrum" — and the Kentucky industrialist Patrick Henry Callahan chairs it. The Order's chosen instrument against the slander campaign is study and publicity, not litigation alone.[45] [44] [1]1914; the year is well attested, but the exact date and venue of the Supreme Council action were not established in the sources reached.
1914 1914-01-30 ? Philadelphia, Pennsylvania, USA Megonegal pleads guilty; "evidence was introduced that the 'fake oath' was utterly false" — Before Judge Robert W. Wilson in Quarter Sessions Court, Megonegal pleads guilty and Stage nolo contendere; the prosecution — the district attorney assisted by Owen J. Roberts, the future Supreme Court justice — asked that sentence be suspended, the defendants pleading good faith in having received the "oath" from The Menace. The court record, per the Order's pamphlet, states that evidence was introduced that the fake oath was utterly false.[26]30 January 1914 per the Order's 1914 pamphlet, which reprints the proceedings; attributed to it.
1917 ? United States The Commission on Religious Prejudices issues its final report — The Commission publishes its reports for 1915–1917 and winds up its work. The Order's own timeline claims that between 1914 and 1917 the number of anti-Catholic publications dropped from sixty to fewer than five; the statistic is the Order's and has not been independently verified here.[30] [1]The Commission's reports were published 1915–1917; the final report is dated 1917.
1917 ? Oklahoma, USA Crane v. State: an appellate court sustains a bogus-oath conviction — The Oklahoma Court of Criminal Appeals affirms Roy Crane's criminal-libel conviction for publishing and distributing a book containing the alleged oath, holding that libel of an identifiable class — the Fourth Degree Knights of the county — is actionable without naming every member, and that it sufficed to prove the purported oath false without disclosing the true obligation. Numerous witnesses had testified to the falsity of the charges.[29]1917; the opinion's exact filing date was not extracted from the court archive.
1917 ? United States and France The K of C Huts of the First World War — After the United States enters the war, the Order opens recreation and welfare centers — the "K of C Huts" — for troops in training camps and at the front, under the motto "Everybody Welcome, Everything Free", open to soldiers regardless of race or creed. The Order's account puts the funds raised by the Knights and allied drives at nearly thirty million dollars; the figure is the Order's own.[1] [7]1917; the hut program began after the U.S. declaration of war on 6 April 1917.

1920 1920-08-28 Rome, Italy Benedict XV receives a Knights delegation — Pope Benedict XV receives 235 Knights in private audience after their unveiling of a Lafayette statue in Metz; at his request the Order later opens five youth recreation centers in Rome (1924–1927), per the Order's timeline.[1]
1921 ? Birmingham, Alabama, USA Fr. James E. Coyle, Knights chaplain, is shot dead by a Klansman — Per the Order's historical account, Methodist minister and Klansman Edwin R. Stephenson shoots Fr. James E. Coyle — a charter member of Mobile Council 666 and chaplain of Birmingham Council 635 — on his rectory porch, an hour after Coyle officiated at the wedding of Stephenson's daughter to a Puerto Rican Catholic. The Klan paid for the defense, which included the future Supreme Court justice Hugo Black, and the jury acquitted. The account and its characterizations are the Order's, in its 2017 institutional history of the Klan conflict.[31]August 1921. 1922 1922-11-07 ? Oregon, USA Oregon passes the Klan-backed Compulsory Education Act — Oregon voters approve an initiative eliminating private schooling for children aged eight to sixteen — a measure campaigned for by the Ku Klux Klan and allied groups, motivated, in the Education Next centennial account's words, by anti-immigrant and anti-Catholic views. The Klan-supported candidate Walter M. Pierce wins the governorship on the same ballot. The law, set to take effect in 1926, would have closed the Catholic schools.[41]7 November 1922, the general-election ballot. 1923 ? Indianapolis, Indiana, USA The Klan press keeps the oath in circulation — The Fiery Cross, the Klan's Indianapolis weekly, engages the Knights of Columbus repeatedly across its 1922–1924 run — the digitized archive returns dozens of full-text matches for the Order and the "alleged" oath. 1923 1923-08-25 Carnegie, Pennsylvania, USA Carnegie repels a Klan march — town residents, not the Order — Some ten thousand Klansmen gather outside Carnegie for an initiation at Imperial Wizard Hiram Evans's behest, then march into the town, where residents — largely Irish Catholics — fight them street by street until Klansman Thomas R. Abbott is shot dead; a local Catholic undertaker was charged and never convicted, and Abbott's death became a Klan recruiting theme. Philip Jenkins's scholarly study treats Carnegie as the signal episode of Pennsylvania's nativist eruption. No reliable source ties the riot to the Knights of Columbus as an organization, and this dataset does not.[40] [43]

E. B. Du Bois's The Gift of Black Folk: The Negroes in the Making of America, George Cohen's The Jews in the Making of America, and Frederick Franklin Schrader's The Germans in the Making of America — a Catholic fraternal order commissioning a founder of the NAACP, in the middle of the Klan's decade, to write Black Americans into the national story.[35] [36] [37] [31]1924 imprints, all three verified in Internet Archive scans. The Order's account says the Historical Commission was established in 1921 and published the series 'three years later'; no 1923 imprint was found.
1924 1924-05-17 South Bend, Indiana, USA Notre Dame students — not the Order — clash with a Klan rally — Over three days, University of Notre Dame students defy their president's directive to stay on campus, misdirect arriving Klansmen, confiscate robes and fight in the streets after D. 1925 1925-06-01 Washington, D.C., USA Pierce v. Society of Sisters strikes down the Oregon law — The Supreme Court unanimously holds Oregon's Compulsory Education Act unconstitutional: "the child is not the mere creature of the State". The Order's own account states that it helped finance the Sisters' litigation after Supreme Knight Flaherty called on members to rally in defense of the Catholic schools; the funding claim is the Order's, with no independent academic corroboration reached in this dataset.[39] [31] [41]
1925 1925-11-14 Noblesville, Indiana, USA The Klan begins to collapse — from its own scandals, the historians say — At the height of its power the second Klan starts to destroy itself: a Hamilton County jury convicts D. C. Stephenson, Grand Dragon of Indiana, of the second-degree murder of Madge Oberholtzer, and the documents he releases from prison in 1926–27 — published by the Indianapolis Times, whose anti-Klan crusade wins the 1928 Pulitzer Prize — bring indictments against Governor Ed Jackson and other Klan-allied officials. Historians' membership estimates trace the fall: from a peak of several million in the mid-1920s (estimates range from about two to six million) to roughly 350,000 by 1927 in Linda Gordon's account and about 30,000 by 1930 in Shawn Lay's. Scholars attribute the collapse to leadership corruption — the Stephenson case above all — feuding, scandal, press exposure and fading novelty, not to physical resistance by its opponents: reviewing the one popular book that argues otherwise, the Indiana-Klan historian Allen Safianow judged that the 1924 South Bend confrontation "made little contribution to the Indiana Klan's precipitous decline". This event stands here as context: the Knights of Columbus fought the Klan chiefly in courtrooms and in print, and no scholarly account reached by this dataset credits the Order — or street confrontation — with the Klan's fall.[47] [48] [49] [50] [51]

Flaherty and other officers meet President Calvin Coolidge about the Calles government's persecution of the Catholic Church in Mexico, and the Order launches a one-million-dollar educational campaign on the crisis — advocacy the Order continued through the following decade.[1]1926; the day of the Coolidge meeting was not established in the sources reached.
1926 1926-11-18 Rome, Italy Pius XI names the Knights in Iniquis Afflictisque — In his encyclical on the persecution of the Church in Mexico, Pius XI singles out the Knights of Columbus by name: members, he writes, "have brought great honor upon themselves", and some were "taken to prison handcuffed, through the public streets". Parallel storylines

The chronology read as parallel storylines: one row per lane, one column per decade, each cell the number of that lane’s events in that decade. An event that belongs to several storylines is counted in each, so the rows sum to more than the number of events. Select a number to jump to that decade in the chronology.

These lanes are an editorial reading of this chronology, not a neutral index. They group events by the kind of act — building the Order, defending it against organized anti-Catholicism, dealing with the Church's hierarchy, and acting in public — and deliberately not by whose account one follows. The anti-Catholic-conflict lane exists because the campaign against the Order is documented in primary sources on both sides and is a declared focus of this project; giving it a lane is a statement that it is part of the story, not a claim about any present-day actor. Most events belong to more than one lane. What was considered and left out, and why, is recorded in the project's issue tracker.

What each lane is grounded in
  1. The Order as an institution — Founding, charters, councils, degrees, membership rules and leadership — the sequence the Order's own records and Kauffman's centennial history 'Faith and Fraternalism' (1982) both treat as the institutional spine.
  2. Anti-Catholic campaigns and the Order's response — The documented conflict between the Order and organized anti-Catholicism in the United States: the fabricated 'bogus oath', the criminal-libel prosecutions, the Order's Commission on Religious Prejudices, the second Ku Klux Klan's campaigns, the Oregon School Law fight, and the oath's later resurfacings. Grounded in the House election-case record of 1913, the Commission's own published reports, and the scholarship on the second Klan.
  3. Relations with the hierarchy and the Holy See — Dated acts of popes, bishops and dicasteries concerning the Order — audiences, the encyclical Iniquis Afflictisque (1926), the canonization path of the founder — each cited to the act itself where reachable.
  4. Public campaigns and service programs — The Order's outward-facing programs as dated public acts — the WWI huts, the 'under God' Pledge campaign, the Ultrasound Initiative, refugee and persecuted-Christians relief — sourced to the Order's record and attributed as such where the Order is the only witness.

Disambiguation & nuance

The Knights of Columbus share the word "Knights" — and, in hostile propaganda, an imagined secret violence — with organizations they have nothing to do with. These distinctions are documented, not polemical.

Not the Sovereign Military Order of Malta

The Order of Malta is an eleventh-century religious and chivalric order with sovereign status in international law. The Knights of Columbus are an American fraternal benefit society founded in 1882. The similarity of style ("Knights", degrees, regalia) is the whole of the resemblance.[21] [5]

Not the Equestrian Order of the Holy Sepulchre

The Holy Sepulchre order is a papal order of knighthood supporting the Church in the Holy Land. It is unrelated to the Knights of Columbus in origin, governance and purpose.[22] [5]

Not the Knights of Labor

The Knights of Labor were a nineteenth-century American labor federation, founded in 1869. Period sources sometimes confuse the two; they are entirely distinct, though both figured in Catholic debates of the 1880s about societies and secrecy.[23]

The "bogus oath" is a fabrication, not a leaked document

The text circulated from 1912 as the Knights' Fourth Degree oath — pledging extermination of Freemasons and heretics — is a fabrication. A committee of the U.S. House of Representatives examined it in a 1913 contested-election case and found it spurious, and publishers who circulated it were convicted of criminal libel. It has nevertheless resurfaced for over a century: the Klan press of the 1920s, the presidential campaigns of 1928 and 1960, and — as an echo of the genre rather than the text — questioning of a judicial nominee's membership in 2018.[25] [32] [33] [46]

The Columbian Squires are the Order's own youth program

The Squires, established in 1925, are part of the Knights of Columbus — not a separate or rival organization.[1]
